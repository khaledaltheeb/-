# Read Together, Grow Together — References for final pages

Status: PREPARATION ONLY — scientific reference library for the Arabic and English booklet editions.

## Editorial rule

The public booklet must remain visually light. Full bibliographic references are **not repeated inside each chapter**. The PDF uses small endnote markers only where a scientific claim needs traceability, while the complete references are grouped in the **final pages of the booklet**.

Arabic and English editions use the same reference numbers so the two versions remain auditable against one evidence map.

Do not cite a source merely because it discusses a similar topic. Each citation must support the exact strength and scope of the public claim.

# المراجع / References

## [1] World Health Organization — early childhood development

World Health Organization. (2020). *Improving early childhood development: WHO guideline*. World Health Organization. ISBN 978-92-4-000209-8.

Primary use: responsive caregiving, early learning, caregiver support, integration of caregiver mental-health support into early-childhood development.

Evidence boundary: supports global, evidence-informed recommendations; it does not mean every household technique in this booklet has been individually tested by WHO.

## [2] WHO, UNICEF, World Bank Group et al. — Nurturing Care Framework

World Health Organization, United Nations Children’s Fund, World Bank Group, ECDAN, PMNCH, & Every Woman Every Child. (2018). *Nurturing care for early childhood development: A framework for helping children survive and thrive to transform health and human potential*. World Health Organization. ISBN 978-92-4-151406-4.

Primary use: safety and security, responsive caregiving, early learning, caregiver-child relationships, holistic early development.

## [3] American Academy of Pediatrics — literacy promotion policy

Klass, P., Miller-Fitzwater, A., High, P. C., & Council on Early Childhood. (2024). Literacy Promotion: An Essential Component of Primary Care Pediatric Practice: Policy Statement. *Pediatrics, 154*(6), e2024069090. https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2024-069090

Primary use: shared reading as a positive caregiver-child practice; relational, language, literacy, cognitive and social-emotional framing.

## [4] American Academy of Pediatrics — literacy technical report

Klass, P., Mendelsohn, A. L., Hutton, J. S., Dunlap, M., Anderson, A. T., High, P. C., Navsaria, D., & Council on Early Childhood. (2024). Literacy Promotion: An Essential Component of Primary Care Pediatric Practice: Technical Report. *Pediatrics, 154*(6), e2024069091. https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2024-069091

Primary use: evidence details behind developmentally appropriate, interactive shared reading and early relational health.

## [5] Shared picture-book reading meta-analysis

Dowdall, N., Melendez-Torres, G. J., Murray, L., Gardner, F., Hartford, L., & Cooper, P. J. (2020). Shared picture book reading interventions for child language development: A systematic review and meta-analysis. *Child Development, 91*(2), e383–e399. https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.13225

Primary use: parent/caregiver book-sharing interventions and expressive/receptive language outcomes; caregiver book-sharing competence.

Evidence boundary: effect sizes are group-level results from interventions; never convert them into a guaranteed individual outcome or a promise tied to a fixed number of books.

## [6] American Academy of Pediatrics — healthy mental and emotional development

Berger-Jenkins, E., Poon, J. K., Davis, K. H., Starin, A., Segal, R., Lavin, A., Committee on Psychosocial Aspects of Child and Family Health, & Section on Developmental and Behavioral Pediatrics. (2026). Framework for Approaching Healthy Mental and Emotional Development in Pediatrics: Clinical Report. *Pediatrics, 157*(5), e2026076620. https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2026-076620

Primary use: strengths-based mental and emotional development, developmental context, stable relationships, positive parenting, routines, age-appropriate choices and iterative support rather than reducing mental health to diagnosis alone.

## [7] Harvard Center on the Developing Child — resilience

National Scientific Council on the Developing Child. (2015). *Supportive relationships and active skill-building strengthen the foundations of resilience: Working Paper No. 13*. Center on the Developing Child at Harvard University.

Primary use: resilience as adaptation supported by stable, committed relationships and active skill building rather than emotional hardness or absence of distress.

## [8] Harvard Center on the Developing Child — serve and return

Center on the Developing Child at Harvard University. (n.d.). *Serve and Return: Back-and-forth exchanges*. Center on the Developing Child at Harvard University.

Primary use: responsive back-and-forth interaction, following a child’s signals, naming what the child sees/does/feels, conversational turn-taking.

Evidence boundary: this is an authoritative translational resource, not a clinical practice guideline; use it to explain mechanisms and implementation simply.

## [9] American Academy of Pediatrics — prevention of child maltreatment

Stirling, J., Gavril, A., Brennan, B., et al. (2024). The Pediatrician’s Role in Preventing Child Maltreatment: Clinical Report. *Pediatrics, 154*(2), e2024067608. https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2024-067608

Primary use: strong, stable, nurturing relationships; prevention framing; caregiver and societal responsibility; safeguarding and protective factors.

Evidence boundary: body-safety education must never imply that a child is responsible for preventing abuse.

## [10] American Academy of Pediatrics — discipline without physical punishment or humiliation

Sege, R. D., Siegel, B. S., Council on Child Abuse and Neglect, & Committee on Psychosocial Aspects of Child and Family Health. (2018). Effective Discipline to Raise Healthy Children. *Pediatrics, 142*(6), e20183112. https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2018-3112

Primary use: positive discipline, limits without physical punishment, yelling, humiliation or shaming.

## [11] Caregiver sensitivity and attachment — 2024 meta-analysis

Madigan, S., Deneault, A.-A., Duschinsky, R., Bakermans-Kranenburg, M. J., Schuengel, C., van IJzendoorn, M. H., Ly, A., Fearon, R. M. P., Eirich, R., & Verhage, M. L. (2024). Maternal and paternal sensitivity: Key determinants of child attachment security examined through meta-analysis. *Psychological Bulletin, 150*(7), 839–872. https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000433

Primary use: careful wording that sensitive caregiving is positively associated with attachment security for both maternal and paternal relationships.

Evidence boundary: sensitivity is important but does not by itself guarantee attachment security or any single developmental outcome.

## [12] Caregiver sensitivity — umbrella review

Madigan, S., et al. (2026). Annual Research Review: The role of caregiver sensitivity in children’s developmental outcomes — an umbrella review. *Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry*.

Primary use: current synthesis of associations between caregiver sensitivity and attachment, cognition, language and socioemotional outcomes.

Editorial note: verify final volume/issue/pages/DOI before the public PDF is typeset; PubMed record is current but final citation metadata must be locked at release.

## [13] Autonomy-supportive parenting across cultures — meta-analysis

Bradshaw, E. L., Duineveld, J. J., Conigrave, J. H., Steward, B. A., Ferber, K. A., Joussemet, M., Parker, P. D., & Ryan, R. M. (2025). Disentangling autonomy-supportive and psychologically controlling parenting: A meta-analysis of self-determination theory’s dual process model across cultures. *American Psychologist, 80*(6), 879–895. https://doi.org/10.1037/amp0001389

Primary use: autonomy support is positively associated with child wellbeing while psychological control is associated with ill-being; effects were broadly observed across regions/cultural dimensions in the synthesis.

Evidence boundary: “supporting autonomy” means supporting age-appropriate volition and initiative, not abandoning parental guidance or transferring adult decisions to a young child.

## [14] Autonomy support, culture and child symptoms — 2026 meta-analysis

Pinquart, M., & Nguyen, V. A. (2026). Cultural Similarities and Differences in the Association of Parental Autonomy Support with Internalizing and Externalizing Problems: A Meta-Analysis. *Journal of Child and Family Studies, 35*, 1141–1152. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10826-026-03281-6

Primary use: cautious cross-cultural framing of autonomy support; distinguish promotion of volitional functioning from simplistic “independence.”

## [15] Family and child routines — systematic review

Selman, S. B., & Dilworth-Bart, J. E. (2024). Routines and child development: A systematic review. *Journal of Family Theory & Review, 16*(2), 272–328. https://doi.org/10.1111/jftr.12549

Primary use: predictable family/child routines and their associations with developmental, social-emotional, self-regulatory, mental-health and physical-health outcomes.

Evidence boundary: most included evidence is associative; do not promise that one routine will prevent a disorder or guarantee a specific outcome.

## [16] Gratitude in early adolescents — randomized classroom experiment

Froh, J. J., Sefick, W. J., & Emmons, R. A. (2008). Counting blessings in early adolescents: An experimental study of gratitude and subjective well-being. *Journal of School Psychology, 46*(2), 213–233. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsp.2007.03.005

Primary use: cautious statement that gratitude practices have shown benefits for some subjective-wellbeing outcomes in an early-adolescent sample.

Evidence boundary: this does **not** justify telling a distressed young child to “be grateful,” nor claiming that gratitude prevents depression or anxiety.

## [17] Augmentative and Alternative Communication

American Speech-Language-Hearing Association. (n.d.). *Augmentative and Alternative Communication (AAC): Practice Portal*. American Speech-Language-Hearing Association.

Primary use: communication can include speech, gestures, signs, objects, pictures and AAC systems; do not define meaningful participation only by spoken responses.

## [18] WHO & UNICEF — nurturing care for children with developmental delays and disabilities

World Health Organization & United Nations Children’s Fund. (2026). *Nurturing care for children with developmental delays and disabilities: Thematic brief*. World Health Organization. ISBN/identifier B09617.

Primary use: inclusive, family-centred nurturing care and the diversity of support needs among children with developmental delays and disabilities.

## [19] E-books and language — meta-analysis

Egert, F., Cordes, A.-K., & Hartig, F. (2022). Can e-books foster child language? Meta-analysis on the effectiveness of e-book interventions in early childhood education and care. *Educational Research Review, 37*, 100472. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2022.100472

Primary use: e-books can support language in some structured early-childhood contexts; repetition, session number and how e-book activities are embedded matter.

Evidence boundary: do not claim all e-books outperform print, do not generalize center-based experimental interventions to every home app, and do not treat passive screen exposure as equivalent to shared reading.

## [20] Social comparison and problematic social-media use — meta-analysis

Demir, M., Erdoğan Yayla, İ., Kaya, M., Koçak, L., Arpaci, I., & Baloglu, M. (2026). Relationships between problematic social media use and social comparison: A meta-analysis. *BMC Psychology*. https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05023-1

Primary use: only for older-child/adolescent digital-comparison content if that material remains in the final edition. The pooled relationship was modest and heterogeneous; adolescent subgroup effects could be small.

Evidence boundary: never say that comparison or social media “causes psychological burnout” in every child.
