# Evidence matrix — Rawafid Family Reading & Wellbeing Companion

Status: working scientific review matrix. This is not a bibliography dump; every row exists to control claim strength and prevent overstatement.

## Evidence grading

- **A**: high-authority guideline/policy statement or directly relevant systematic review/meta-analysis.
- **B**: converging review evidence, strong observational/longitudinal evidence or meta-analysis supporting the broader construct but not every exact household technique.
- **C**: low-risk practice recommendation derived from developmental principles; present as a suggestion, not an outcome guarantee.
- **Reject/Rewrite**: statement is too causal, universal, diagnostic, stigmatizing or unsupported.

| Domain | Publishable claim / framing | Grade | Preferred evidence basis | What not to claim |
|---|---|---:|---|---|
| Shared reading | Shared reading is a useful way to support language, learning and caregiver-child interaction when the adult remains responsive and conversational. | A | AAP literacy-promotion guidance; systematic reviews/meta-analyses of shared book reading | A fixed number of books guarantees intelligence, school success or mental-health protection. |
| Responsive interaction | Young children develop within responsive relationships; noticing cues, responding, taking turns and expanding interaction are developmentally important. | A | WHO Nurturing Care; Harvard Center on the Developing Child “serve and return” framework | A single technique creates secure attachment or prevents mental illness. |
| Co-regulation | Young children often need a calm, responsive adult to help them manage intense emotion before independent self-regulation is realistic. | A/B | Early-childhood mental-health / responsive caregiving guidance | “A well-parented child should calm themselves quickly.” |
| Resilience | Resilience is supported by protective relationships and opportunities to cope and recover; it is not the absence of distress. | A/B | Harvard resilience framework and developmental literature | “Strong children do not cry,” “ignore sadness to build toughness.” |
| Attachment + autonomy | Warm, sensitive caregiving and age-appropriate autonomy support can coexist; security does not require dependence or unquestioning compliance. | A/B | Attachment meta-analytic literature; autonomy-support meta-analyses | “A securely attached child always obeys” or “independence means less attachment.” |
| Repair after conflict | Caregivers can acknowledge mistakes, restore connection and re-state limits; presenting this as a relationship practice is appropriate. | B/C | Parent-child relationship / emotion-socialization literature | Claim that apologizing after every conflict prevents psychopathology. |
| Comparison/self-worth | Frequent evaluative comparison can focus a child on ranking rather than effort, values or individual progress; avoid habitual sibling or appearance comparisons. | B/C | Social-comparison and self-evaluation literature; developmentally cautious application | “Comparison causes burnout in all children.” |
| Gratitude | Gratitude exercises may support aspects of wellbeing in some populations; with young children use simple noticing/appreciation practices without denying difficult emotions. | B/C | Youth gratitude intervention literature and reviews | Gratitude prevents depression/anxiety, or a sad child should simply “be grateful.” |
| Contentment | Frame contentment as noticing enoughness and appreciation while preserving goals, curiosity and effort. | C | Developmentally coherent practice; do not medicalize | “Contentment lowers ambition” or “content children never envy.” |
| Emotion validation | Naming and acknowledging emotion can be paired with behavioral limits: all feelings can be discussed; not every behavior is allowed. | A/B | Emotion-socialization / pediatric mental-health frameworks | Validating a feeling means allowing aggression or removing every limit. |
| Body autonomy | Teach anatomically accurate or clear body-part language, privacy, boundaries and that children can report unwanted/confusing touch. | A | AAP child maltreatment prevention guidance and safeguarding practice | The child is responsible for preventing abuse. |
| “Stranger danger” | Safety rules should not be limited to strangers; boundary violations can involve familiar people too. | A | Child abuse prevention guidance | “Only strangers are dangerous.” |
| Touch context | Explain that some touch can be necessary for care, hygiene or health, but should be explained and handled respectfully; a child can report discomfort or confusion. | A/B | Pediatric safeguarding guidance | A simplistic red/green body map with no context, or “no one may ever touch these areas” without care/medical context. |
| Disclosure | If a child reports harm or discomfort, respond calmly, believe/receive the disclosure, avoid blame, and involve appropriate safeguarding pathways. | A | Safeguarding standards | Interrogate the child repeatedly or ask leading questions. |
| Trusted adults | Teach children more than one trusted adult and permission to keep telling if the first person does not help. | B/C | Common safeguarding implementation practice | Guarantee that a nominated adult will always be safe; adults still need vetting and safeguarding responsibility. |
| Social confidence | Children can learn simple joining, greeting, turn-taking, help-seeking and assertive-boundary language through modeling and practice. | B/C | Social competence / parenting intervention literature | A quiet child is defective; sociability should be forced. |
| People-pleasing | Kindness and cooperation do not require surrendering bodily boundaries or agreeing to everything adults/peers request. | C | Safeguarding + autonomy principles | “Good children always do what adults say.” |
| Family belonging | Predictable warmth, shared routines, family stories and participation can support a sense of belonging and connection. | B/C | Family routines / relationship literature | “Family loyalty” requires secrecy, silence about harm or taking parents’ side against safety. |
| Respect for parents | Parent authority can be framed as guidance, care and boundaries while preserving the child’s dignity and voice. | B/C | Positive parenting / autonomy-support literature | Humiliation, fear, physical punishment or unquestioning obedience as markers of respect. |
| Inclusive communication | Communication can include speech, gesture, pointing, pictures and AAC; shared reading should allow multiple response modes. | A/B | Communication-disability and inclusive-practice guidance | Spoken answers are required for engagement or comprehension. |
| Eye contact | Do not require eye contact as proof of attention or respect, especially for children with neurodevelopmental or sensory differences. | B/C | Autism/neurodiversity-informed practice | “Look at me when I speak or you are not listening.” |
| Sensory adaptation | Quiet environments, shorter sessions, predictable routines and repetition can make shared reading more accessible for some children. | B/C | Inclusive / developmental practice | These adaptations are necessary for every autistic or disabled child. |
| Repetition | Re-reading can support participation, familiarity and language learning; it need not be treated as a failure to progress. | A/B | Early-literacy literature | Repetition is always superior to new books. |
| Digital shared reading | Digital reading can still be relational when the adult converses and shares attention; the device should not automatically replace interaction. | B | Digital shared-reading literature; campaign implementation | Any screen-based reading is automatically equivalent to print or automatically harmful. |

## Core sources to anchor the manuscript

### Normative / professional guidance

1. **World Health Organization — Nurturing Care / responsive caregiving**. Use for responsive relationships, caregiver wellbeing, early learning and development framing.
2. **American Academy of Pediatrics — Literacy Promotion: An Essential Component of Primary Care Pediatric Practice**. Use for shared reading, relational health and literacy promotion.
3. **American Academy of Pediatrics — child maltreatment prevention / pediatrician’s role in preventing child abuse and neglect**. Use for body safety, privacy, disclosure and caregiver responsibilities.
4. **Harvard Center on the Developing Child — Serve and Return; Resilience**. Use as an implementation-friendly conceptual source, while distinguishing it from clinical guidelines.

### Reviews / syntheses to verify during manuscript drafting

- Shared book reading effects on expressive/receptive language and interaction.
- Sensitive caregiving and attachment security.
- Parental autonomy support and child/adolescent wellbeing.
- Gratitude interventions in children/adolescents: modest, population-dependent effects; avoid causal overreach.
- Social comparison and wellbeing/self-evaluation: especially careful with age, platform use and causality.

## Claim-language controls

### Preferred

- “قد يساعد / may support”
- “يرتبط / is associated with”
- “توصي الإرشادات / guidance recommends”
- “جرّب / try”
- “لبعض الأطفال / for some children”
- “بحسب العمر والسياق / depending on age and context”

### Restricted

Do not use without unusually strong evidence and precise context:

- يمنع / prevents
- يضمن / guarantees
- يسبب / causes
- يعالج / treats
- يحمي من كل / protects from all
- يبني شخصية قوية / builds a strong personality (too vague)
- يمنع الهشاشة النفسية / prevents psychological fragility (nonstandard and stigmatizing)

## Safeguarding red lines

- Never teach that abuse occurs because a child failed to say no, escape or tell early enough.
- Never imply relatives, teachers, professionals or known adults are automatically safe.
- Never ask the child to keep a family secret that involves fear, touching, threats or harm.
- Never publish a child story/image without documented authorization and an appropriate release process.
- Never use child body-safety content to create fear of normal care, affection or healthcare; teach context and the right to speak about discomfort.
- Never make a child responsible for preserving family reputation over safety.

## Manuscript-review checklist

For each chapter, reviewers must be able to mark YES to all:

- Is the developmental age/context clear?
- Is the strongest claim supported by a traceable source?
- Is causality weaker or equal to what the evidence permits?
- Is there a useful action, phrase or reading prompt?
- Could the wording accidentally shame a child or caregiver?
- Could the wording silence disclosure or encourage blind obedience?
- Is disability/neurodiversity handled without deficit assumptions?
- Is the section readable without specialist vocabulary?
- Could a caregiver apply it in under five minutes?
- Would the same canonical meaning survive Arabic/English publication?
